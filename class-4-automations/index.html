2 / What this is
Why this class exists

An automation does the busywork — so you don't.

You already know what these are — you just do them by hand. Think about the little office jobs that eat your day and don't really need you, just your time. Each of these can run on its own:

📥 Sort my inbox

Instead of scanning a hundred messages every morning, it reads them and tells you the few that actually need you today.

✍️ Draft the reply

For the emails you answer the same way every time, it writes the reply in your words — you just read it and hit send.

📝 Summarize the thread

A 20-message back-and-forth becomes three lines: what was decided, and what's next — without you reading all of it.

🔔 Chase the follow-ups

It remembers who never replied and sends the nudge, so you're not keeping a mental list of loose ends.

Notice what they share: each one reads something and makes a small judgment — what's urgent, how to reply, what matters. That "deciding" is the interesting part, and it's exactly what today's build does.

Read your sources → decide what matters → deliver.

Every automation is the same three-part shape, and yours is no exception. The middle step is the interesting one: most of the script is plain, predictable code, but the moment where it has to judge what's important, it hands that one question to Claude and takes the answer back.

Plain code does the reading and the delivering — those are mechanical. The LLM is called only at the judgment step, where fixed rules can't tell "urgent" from "noise." That split — rules around a spot of judgment — is exactly what today builds.

5 / Pluggable
Build it as parts, not one blob

Each source is its own little connector.

The script isn't one big tangle. It's a set of small, separate readers — one for your calendar, one for the news, one for your tasks — each a self-contained piece that fetches its thing and hands back clean results. You start with a couple and plug in more whenever you want.

✅ Start here today (no accounts to wrangle)
  • Your calendar — today's events, via a private link.
  • News on a topic you pick — your company, a competitor, your field.
  • Your website's status — is your booking/checkout link up?
🔌 Plug in later (one copy-paste key each)
  • Your task manager (Todoist) — today's + overdue tasks.
  • Slack — recent messages that mention you.
  • Your email — the inbox worth triaging.

Why build it in separate pieces? Two reasons: you can add a new source without touching the others — and next class, these same readers become the tools your agent uses. Today you're quietly building its senses.

6 / Runs without you
The part that makes it an automation

It runs on a schedule — on your own computer.

No website, no cloud service to rent. The script lives on your Mac (or PC), and your computer's own built-in scheduler wakes it up at the time you choose. That's the whole "without you" part — you set it once and it just happens each morning.

⏰ The built-in scheduler

Every Mac and PC ships with one — a quiet clock that runs programs at set times. Claude sets it up for you: "run this script at 7am every day." On Mac it's called launchd; on Windows, Task Scheduler. You never touch the details.

😴 The one honest catch

It runs when your computer is on. Asleep at 7am? The scheduler remembers, and runs it the moment you wake the machine — so you still get your brief, just a little later. (Off all weekend is the one real gap — a good reason later work moves off your laptop.)

This is genuinely different from Class 2's cloud pipeline: that lived on a server; this lives on your own machine, doing a chore for you. Simpler, free, and entirely yours.

7 / Plan mode
A habit you know — use it again

Use plan mode — let Claude plan before it builds.

Same habit from Class 3. For a job with this many moving parts — several readers, a judgment step, delivery, a schedule — don't let Claude charge straight in. Switch to plan mode first: it thinks the whole thing through and hands you a plan to approve before a single file is written.

How to turn it on

Press Shift+Tab to cycle the modes and stop on Plan mode. Describe what you want — Claude proposes, you approve or push back.

Why it's worth it

Build on the fly and Claude guesses at each step — more mistakes, more debugging. Plan first and it reasons about the whole job up front, so you catch a wrong turn while it's still words on a screen.

📖

Plans come out technical by default — make Claude write them in plain English. Add "explain it like I'm not technical" to your request, or set that rule once in your CLAUDE.md so every plan is readable before you approve.

8 / Reference documents
Your project's knowledge base

Reference documents — a Wikipedia for your project.

Claude doesn't remember yesterday's chat. Reference documents are plain files in your project that record how it works — what each reader does, how it's scheduled, why it's built that way. So you (or Claude in a fresh session) can read up and get the full picture — and you get most of them for free.

During the build → the plan doc

The plan from plan mode is a reference doc. Claude saves it and works against it to stay on task. No extra effort from you — approving the plan already created it.

At the very end → the architecture doc

When it's built, have Claude write an architecture doc: what each reader pulls, where the judgment step lives, how it's scheduled. This is the one doc worth asking for explicitly.

💡

Why it matters here especially: next class you'll re-open this project and turn these readers into an agent's tools. A good architecture doc means Claude instantly knows how everything fits — instead of relearning your script from scratch.

10 / Plan step 1 · The readers
What the plan does first — no prompt to paste

Step 1 — build a reader for each source.

You approved the plan, so Claude works through it — you don't paste anything more. First it builds the readers: a small, separate function per source, each one fetching its thing and handing back clean results. Here's what the three starters pull, and why none of them need a painful sign-in.

📅 Calendar

Google Calendar gives you a private link to your own schedule (Settings → "Secret address in iCal format"). The script just fetches it — no sign-in flow, no setup project. Copy the link once, done.

📰 News

A Google News feed for any phrase you pick — your company, a rival, your industry. Just a web address the script reads. Nothing to sign into at all.

🌐 Site status

A quick check that your most important link — booking page, checkout, storefront — is actually up. One line: is it answering, or is it down?

Notice the pattern: each is its own function. That's deliberate — a clean reader you can test on its own, add to, and (next class) hand to an agent as a tool.

11 / Plan step 2 · The judgment
The one step that needs a brain

Step 2 — hand the pile to Claude to sort.

The readers return a messy pile of everything. This step is the one place fixed rules fail — "which of these actually matters?" needs judgment — so the script hands the pile to Claude and gets back a ranked, sorted brief. To make that call, the script needs to reach Claude, and there are two ways.

🆓 Free — use the Claude you already pay for

Your computer already has Claude Code signed in. Run claude setup-token once — it makes a one-year pass the script uses to reach Claude on your existing subscription. No extra bill. This is the path we'll use today.

🔑 Bulletproof — its own key

The other way is an API key (a card the script carries). Costs pennies a month for a daily brief, never expires quietly. You don't need it today — but remember it: next class, your agent will need its own key.

💡

This is the seam to the agents class. Today your script borrows the Claude you pay for — it runs as you, for you. Next class the agent gets its own key, because the moment software acts on its own, it can't ride your personal login. Same idea, one step further.

12 / Plan step 3 · Deliver + schedule
Send it, and set it loose

Step 3 — email the brief, then set the alarm.

Two small pieces to finish. Claude wires both — you just approve when it asks.

📬 Deliver it to you

The brief goes out as a plain email to yourself — the simplest delivery there is, no domain or setup. (Later you can send it to Slack, or as a phone notification — same brief, different doorway.)

⏰ Schedule it

Claude sets your computer's built-in scheduler to run the script every morning at your time, and to catch up on wake if the machine was asleep. Tell it the time you want — that's your only input.

🔐

One habit worth setting now: any keys for other services (your task manager, mail) go in a small .env file that never gets uploaded to GitHub — not pasted into the script. Claude sets this up correctly; just know that's why it's there.

The plan's last step: don't wait until tomorrow morning to find out if it works. Run the script by hand, in the room, and watch the brief land in your inbox. Then confirm the schedule is set so it fires on its own from now on.

Something off — brief empty, email didn't arrive, a reader errored? Tell Claude exactly what you saw; it diagnoses and re-runs. Same "describe it to Claude" habit from every class.

14 / Make it act
From "tells you" to "does a little"

Have it do things — safely, on your terms.

A brief that only tells you is a great start. The next step is having it take a small action for you — and there are two that are genuinely useful and stay completely safe, because they never send or delete anything on your behalf.

✍️ Draft, don't send

It reads the email worth a reply and writes you a draft — you approve and send it yourself, or tweak it first. The hard part (the writing) is done; you keep the final click.

✅ File it to your task list

It turns "the three things that need doing" into actual tasks in Todoist — on your list, ready, before you sat down.

🚀

Everything past that — send the email as you, move a calendar event, post to Slack — is reachable, and you now have the skills. We don't build it live (it means giving a script real power over your accounts), but it's the natural next frontier, and it's where the agents class heads.

15 / When it breaks
A machine you trust has to be honest

Make it tell you when it breaks.

An automation you rely on has one real danger: it stops working and you don't notice. A briefing that silently dies is worse than no briefing, because you've stopped checking by hand. So the last thing worth building is a script that's honest about its own health.

📣 It emails you when it fails

If a reader errors or Claude can't be reached, the script sends a plain "your briefing broke — here's why" instead of just going dark. You always know it's alive.

☀️ Silence never means "fine"

It sends even on a quiet day — "all clear, nothing urgent" — so no-email always means something's wrong, never "all good." Ask Claude to build in both.

This is the difference between a toy and a tool: logging what it did, and never lying to you by staying silent. It's a small ask of Claude and it's what makes the thing trustworthy enough to actually rely on.
