The one idea behind today

An automation follows steps. An agent decides them.

Last class you wrote the steps yourself: pull these emails, pull today's calendar, sort, write. The same steps every morning, no matter what arrived. That's an automation. An agent gets a goal instead, and works out the steps once it sees what's in front of it.

⚙️ Automation — your morning brief

You decide the steps, in advance. It runs on a schedule and does exactly what you wrote. Perfect for a job that's the same every time.

🧠 Agent — today's build

It decides the steps, as it goes. You give it the goal; it picks what to look at and what to do. Needed for a job that's different every time.

🎯

The test: can you write the steps down before you see what arrives? If yes, it's an automation — keep it one. If the next step depends on what it finds, that's a job for an agent.

Why handling the brief needs an agent

Same inbox, three messages, three different paths.

Look at what it takes to handle three items from one morning's brief. No single recipe covers all three — each one needs a different thing looked up before it can be answered:

📅 "Can we move Thursday?"

It checks your calendar for open slots, then drafts a reply offering two times.

📎 "Following up on the proposal"

It checks your tasks, sees the proposal is overdue, and flags it to you — only you can fix that.

📝 "Did you see my notes?"

It finds the doc they shared, summarizes it, and drafts a reply with your next step.

That's the difference in practice. You could never write one set of steps for "handle whatever came in" — but you can tell an agent what good looks like and let it choose.

When you say "handle #2," it follows the link to the actual thread. No copying, no pasting, no describing the situation from memory.

Swap out last class's instructions

Step 1 — give it its directions.

Last class, your project's instructions only said what matters for the brief. Today they become the agent's directions. Open your Morning Briefing project's instructions, replace what's there with this, and fill in the brackets:

Who you are: my assistant for [my business: what I do and who my customers are].

My morning brief: each morning a brief is saved in this project. Before you act, read the newest one. When I say "#2," I mean item 2 in today's brief. Follow each item's link to the original email, meeting, or task before you act on it.

What you can do: draft email replies for me to approve, file tasks, and write prep notes in our conversation. Never send an email. Never delete anything. If you're not sure, ask me.

How I work: [how I sound when I write, e.g. short and friendly, signed with my first name]. [Who gets priority, e.g. clients get a same-day reply]. [What always comes to me, e.g. anything about money or contracts].
✏️

This is a starting point — make it yours. Edit any line to fit how your business actually works: add rules, cut ones that don't apply, change what it's allowed to do.

Then check it took: with Cowork selected in the message box, ask "Which item in today's brief should I deal with first, and why?" It should name a real item and give a reason you'd agree with.

Last class, everything that writes was set to never

Step 2 — loosen its limits, on purpose.

Your directions say what it may do; these settings make sure it can't do more. In the message box, click + → Connectors → Manage connectors, then click a connector. Every action it can take has its own setting: allow it, ask you first, or never.

✉️ Gmail — set this one first

Set send email to never. Allow creating drafts, so it can write replies for you. Anything that deletes stays never.

⚙️ The rest is yours

Go through each connector and set each action the way you want it: allow what you're comfortable with, ask for anything you want to see first, never for anything you don't want it doing.

🔒

The control is the send button. It can write the draft, but it can't send it — the draft waits in your Gmail until you read it and press send yourself.

The moment the class is for

Step 3 — hand it real work.

Pick three items from this morning's brief and hand them over one at a time. Watch how it chooses what to look at — that's the agent deciding its own steps:

  • 1. "Prep me for my 2pm." → it reads the invite, finds the email thread, checks open tasks with that person, and writes you a one-page prep note right in the conversation.
  • 2. "Draft a reply to #2." → it reads the thread, looks up what the reply needs, and leaves a draft in your Gmail. You read it and send it.
  • 3. "Turn #4 into a task due Friday." → it writes the task with the context attached and files it on your list.
✅

A draft waiting in Gmail, a task on your list, a prep note ready — work you pointed at, done without you doing it.

Open Gmail and your task app and look. Seeing the draft sitting there, written the way you'd write it, is the point of the whole class.

So you don't correct the same thing twice

Step 4 — correct it once, not every time.

It will get small things wrong: offering a Friday you never take, emailing someone who prefers a call. When it does, don't just fix the draft — tell it, and ask it to remember:

Dana prefers a phone call to email for anything about timelines. Remember that.
🧠 It saves it to memory

Start a new conversation tomorrow and ask about Dana: it still knows. That's memory — what it learned from you, kept between conversations.

📄 Instructions or memory?

A rule for everyone ("money questions come to me") goes in your instructions. A fact it learned ("Dana prefers calls") goes in memory.

Look through what it has remembered now and then. If something's wrong or out of date, delete it.

A mistake is information

When it gets it wrong, fix the cause.

Fixing the draft fixes today. Fixing the cause fixes every day after. First ask it "why did you do that?" — then fix the right thing:

📄 A rule was missing

It didn't know how you handle this kind of thing. Add it to your instructions.

🧠 A fact was wrong

It believed something that isn't true. Correct it and have it update its memory.

🔒 It could do too much

It tried something it shouldn't be able to. Tighten that permission.

Describe what went wrong, not how to fix it. "That reply was too formal for a client I've known for years" teaches it more than rewriting the email yourself.

It still waits for you to ask.

Today's agent is capable, but it only works when you open the app and hand it something. You still read the brief first, you still decide what to hand over, and nothing happens while you're busy.

Your homework · do it this week

Grow it this week — your choice.

The goal before next class: an agent you trust with the work you hand it — one you rarely have to correct. Pick the direction that gets you there fastest for your business:

🔁 Give it more automations

Add another scheduled task to the same project — a weekly wrap-up, a Friday check on unpaid invoices, a Monday plan — so there's more for the agent to work from.

☀️ Expand the morning brief

Add a source, sharpen what counts as urgent, or change the sections, so the brief — and the agent's to-do list — fits your day better.

✏️ Keep refining the agent

Hand it items every morning. Each time you correct it, ask why, then fix the cause in your directions or its memory.

📝

You're there when you send its drafts with barely a change. Along the way, keep one list: what you ask it to do most often. Those repeat jobs are where next class starts.
