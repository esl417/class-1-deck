Why reports never fit

Built for everyone. So they fit no one.

It isn't that your reporting tools are bad. It's that they were built for every business at once, and yours is one business.

Three things they all do
  • Standard for everybody. The same screens ship to a florist and a law firm.
  • A learning curve first. You climb the menus before you get the view you wanted.
  • Their words, not yours. Sessions, channels, events. You translate in your head every time.
And the real problem underneath

The number you actually run on is usually computed from two or three others.

No tool ships it, because no tool knows which two you'd pick. So you do the arithmetic in your head, or in a spreadsheet on a Sunday, or you don't do it at all.

You have almost certainly done this: exported a report, then immediately started dividing one column by another to get the thing you actually wanted.

The idea

Don't build a report. Build a decision tool.

A report tells you what happened. A decision tool tells you whether that's good, and what to do next. Almost every dashboard ever made is the first kind.

A report says
  • "You served 240 covers."
  • "You were open 60 hours."
  • "Here are nine more charts."

All true. None of it tells you whether to do anything differently on Monday.

A decision tool says
  • "You're serving 4 covers an hour."
  • "Tuesday nights run at half that."
  • "You're paying a full kitchen for a quiet room. Close Tuesdays or fill them."

Every number carries a verdict.

Every business runs on three or four of these. "Do I spend more on ads, or is that money better somewhere else." "Do I chase the quiet clients, or let them go." "Do I need to hire, or am I just badly organised." You don't answer them once — you answer them again every time something moves, which is why what you're building is a standing view, not a report on last month.

If you don't know which decisions you make, that's normal and it isn't a problem. Ask Claude to propose the metrics worth watching for a business like yours, then pick from the list. Choosing is easier than inventing.

Multiply one by the other and a number I can't act on becomes one I can. That's the whole move — and no tool ships the result, because half of it was never in a system. It was in my head.

So what does 112 returns actually cost me? That's the number I asked for, and it's what we're about to go and look at.

My 5.6 spare hours buy 136 more email orders a week — or 26 of paid search. Same hours, very different answers.

And the part that makes it an instrument rather than a report: none of those conclusions are written down. The four numbers at the top are boxes I can type in — change 40 minutes to 35 and every figure re-reads. Hand it next month's export and it re-reads again: if email becomes my problem channel, the screen says email, and tells me to grow the one that used to be the villain.

That's the difference between a report and an instrument. A report is the answer to one Monday's question. This asks the question again every week, and it's allowed to change its mind.
