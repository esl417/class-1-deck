# Teaching notes — Lightning Lesson (Stop Winging It: Watch a Real Business Strategy Get Built)

Per-slide notes, keyed by slide label (`##` = the slide's `data-label`). Written
for the person reading this deck AFTER the session, with Claude, trying to judge
or rebuild the strategy for their own business.

**This session was lecture and a read-through. Nobody built along, and nothing
was generated live.** Eric ran the strategy in StratEngine before the session
and read the finished document on screen. So a reader arriving here has watched
a strategy being read. They have not written one. Never speak as though they
already have a diagnosis or a set of choices.

**What they are trying to do, end to end:**

1. Run the six-question test on whatever plan they have now (four from "The
   test", two from "Plan and measure").
2. Write one honest sentence naming what is hard about their business right now.
   This is the diagnosis, and it is the step people skip.
3. Look at that difficulty through at least two lenses, not one.
4. Make a small number of choices, each of which costs something.
5. Give each choice an owner, a budget, a date, one early number and one result
   number.

If someone lands here without a specific question, that sequence is the answer.
Start wherever they really are, which is usually step 2.

**Who they are:** non-technical small business owners. Strategy has been made to
sound like MBA territory, and many of them believe it is something large
companies do and they cannot. The governing test for every answer: does this
leave them calmer and more capable, or further behind? Translate every term. If
you use a word like "positioning" or "wholesale", say what it means in a shop.

**The one framing that must not drift:**

> The free lesson teaches *I can tell a real strategy from a wish list, and I
> know what goes into one.* The course sells *the business that carries the
> strategy out.*

The limit is that **a strategy decides and does nothing.** Name that once, on
"What you own", and close it once, on "What's next". Do not explain on "What you

## The drawer plan

The registration page promised this slide: "The plan in your drawer was never a
strategy." The relief matters more than the definition. They were right not to
open the plan again, and nobody taught them the difference.

The left card is Roger Martin's description of a typical strategic plan: a
vision or mission statement, a list of initiatives whose length is "generally
constrained only by affordability", and the conversion of those initiatives into
financials. His point is that such a plan "isn't explicit about what the
organization chooses not to do and why."

The right card is the working definition for the whole session: a few choices
about where you will win, what you will not do, and a bet you could be wrong
about. Martin: "True strategy is about placing bets and making hard choices."

The quote is verbatim from Michael Porter, "What Is Strategy?", Harvard Business
Review, November 1996. The sentence before it in the original is "Strategy is
making trade-offs in competing."

If someone says "but I do have goals": goals are fine, and they are the
destination. The test two slides on covers this. Do not pre-empt it here.

Sources: Martin, "The Big Lie of Strategic Planning", HBR Jan 2014. Porter, HBR
Nov 1996.

## Why they pay for it

This slide exists because the audience does not believe strategy is worth money.
The proof is that the largest companies pay full-time teams to do nothing else.

The figures are from BCG's survey of 48 chief strategy officers (Kachaner and
Stewart, 2013): 84% are responsible for identifying growth opportunities, 82%
for strategic planning, 82% for mergers, acquisitions and divestments. "About
ten people" comes from BCG's 2025 study of more than 430 strategy organizations,
where the typical central team had 11 full-time staff. The 2013 survey found a
median of seven. Say "about ten" and do not be more precise than that.

HBR's 2007 piece on the chief strategy officer gives the plainest reason the job
exists: CEOs "are too overloaded to stay on top of strategy implementation."

**Do not quote a percentage of companies that have a chief strategy officer.**
"About half the Fortune 500" circulates widely and could not be traced to a
source.

The Nike line: the direct-selling push ("Consumer Direct Acceleration") launched
June 2020. Fiscal 2025 revenue was $46.3B, down 10%. Elliott Hill replaced John
Donahoe as CEO in October 2024, and on his first earnings call said partners
"feel we've turned our back on them" and committed to rebuilding wholesale.
Avoid saying the pivot *cost* a specific dollar figure. The "$2 billion" number
attached to it comes from a shareholder lawsuit, not from Nike.

The registration page says "Not a McDonald's case study." This slide uses big
companies as evidence the work is real, then turns straight back to the room:
same kind of choice, less room to be wrong. Keep that turn. It is the point.

## The test

Richard Rumelt's four hallmarks of bad strategy, from "The Perils of Bad
Strategy" (McKinsey Quarterly, June 2011), adapted from *Good Strategy / Bad
Strategy*. His wording for the four: "the failure to face the challenge,
mistaking goals for strategy, bad strategic objectives, and fluff."

- **Fluff.** "A restatement of the obvious, combined with a generous sprinkling
  of buzzwords." The bank example is his: "Our fundamental strategy is one of
  customer-centric intermediation," which he unpacks as the bank's strategy
  being to be a bank.
- **No problem named.** "A strategy is a way through a difficulty, an approach
  to overcoming an obstacle, a response to a challenge." And: "if you fail to
  identify and analyze the obstacles, you don't have a strategy. Instead, you
  have a stretch goal or a budget or a list of things you wish would happen."
- **Goals dressed up as strategy.** His example is a CEO with a "20/20 plan":
  20% revenue growth, 20% margin, and no answer to "what has to happen for it to
  be realized?"
- **A long to-do list.** "A long list of things to do, often mislabeled as
  strategies or objectives, is not a strategy." The city plan with 47 strategies
  and 178 action items, where action item 122 was "create a strategic plan", is
  his real example.

The heads-up line ("adjectives where the choices should be") echoes the
registration page: "Goals with adjectives and a list of hopes are the tells."

The most useful thing to do for a student here is run the test on their actual
plan with them. Ask them to paste it. Most small-business plans fail "no problem
named" first, because naming the difficulty feels like admitting weakness.
Reassure them: large companies fail it just as often, which is why Rumelt wrote
the book.

## Plan and measure

Eric's addition to Rumelt's four, and the bridge to the rest of the course. A
strategy is only as good as its implementation plan, and progress has to be
measurable in concrete terms.

The strategy literature agrees, so this is not a bolt-on. Rumelt's third element
is "coherent actions: steps that are coordinated with one another to support the
accomplishment of the guiding policy," and he closes by calling strategy
"coherent action backed by an argument." The fifth choice in Lafley and Martin's
*Playing to Win* is "What management systems are required?", which includes
"systems to measure attainment of its goals."

The survey figures are from Sull, Homkes and Sull, "Why Strategy Execution
Unravels, and What to Do About It," HBR March 2015 (roughly 7,600 managers in
262 companies). Verbatim: "Only 55% of the middle managers we have surveyed can
name even one of their company's top five priorities." And: "Only 11% of the
managers we have surveyed believe that all their company's strategic priorities
have the financial and human resources needed for success."

"One early signal, one result" is leading versus lagging measures. Kaplan and
Norton: "Outcome measures without performance drivers do not communicate how the
outcomes are to be achieved. They also do not provide an early indication about
whether the strategy is being implemented successfully." For a small business:
the result might be monthly revenue from a new customer type, and the early
signal might be how many of them booked a first call this week.

**Two things never to say here**, because both are false and both are
everywhere:

- "90% of strategies fail" or "only 10% get executed." It traces to a 1982
  *Fortune* article reporting a consultant's informal estimate. The
  peer-reviewed review (Cândido and Santos, 2015) concludes "the true rate of
  implementation failure remains to be determined." The defensible line, from
  HBR, is that two-thirds to three-quarters of large organizations struggle to
  implement their strategies.
- "You can't manage what you can't measure," attributed to Drucker. The Drucker
  Institute says he never said it, and Deming called the idea "a costly myth."
  What Kaplan and Norton did write is "What you measure is what you get."

## Three lenses

Frame a framework as **a question somebody already worked out how to ask.**
That one sentence removes most of the intimidation.

Eric's two caveats are both on the slide and both matter: there are dozens of
frameworks, and each one is used for more than one purpose. These three are the
most common, and nobody should leave thinking they are the full set. The
walkthrough later uses a fourth (Playing to Win), which makes the point by
itself.

Do not teach the lenses as boxes to fill in. The pitfalls slide is about exactly
that failure.

## Five Forces

Michael Porter, first published in HBR in 1979 and updated in "The Five
Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy," HBR January 2008. Its purpose in his
words: understanding the forces "reveals the roots of an industry's current
profitability while providing a framework for anticipating and influencing
competition (and profitability) over time."

The five: threat of entry, power of suppliers, power of buyers, threat of
substitutes, rivalry among existing competitors. A substitute "performs the same
or a similar function as an industry's product by a different means." His
examples: videoconferencing for travel, plastic for aluminum.

**The lens is about the industry, never about the business.** That is the single
most common confusion, and it is why this slide says "knows nothing about you"
two slides later. If a student starts listing their own strengths inside a Five
Forces analysis, they have switched to SWOT without noticing.

The coffee shop is an illustration written for this deck, not a Porter example,
though he does use specialty coffee to make the low-entry-barrier point about
Starbucks. When helping a student apply it, the first job is drawing the
industry boundary sensibly. Porter names "defining the industry too broadly or
too narrowly" as the first pitfall. "Food and beverage" is too broad for a cafe.
"Pour-over cafes on my street" is too narrow.

## SWOT

Origin: the Stanford Research Institute in the 1960s. A 2023 archival study
(Puyt, Lie and Wilderom, *Long Range Planning*) traces it to Robert Franklin
Stewart's team, where it began as SOFT: Satisfactory, Opportunities, Faults,
Threats. Each manager wrote down eight to ten key planning issues and graded
each one "with evidence."

**Do not say Albert Humphrey invented it.** That is the popular story, and the
archival paper contradicts it. "It came out of Stanford Research Institute in
the 1960s" is safe.

The logic, as Hill and Westbrook put it, is fit between "the external situation
a firm faces (threats and opportunities) and its own internal qualities or
characteristics (strengths and weaknesses)."

The cell text says "better than a named rival" on purpose. A strength only means
something against a specific competitor and a specific goal. "Great customer
service" with no comparison is the three-word phrase the pitfalls slide warns
about.

The evidence requirement is the hook into the next slide but one. Land it and
move on.

## Blue Ocean

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, *Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005). The authors'
definition: "The simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and low cost to open up
a new market space and create new demand." The aim is "uncontested market
space, thereby making the competition irrelevant."

The grid on the slide is their four actions framework: Eliminate, Reduce, Raise,
Create. The reason cutting matters is on the slide: what you eliminate and
reduce is what pays for what you create. That is how you get differentiation and
lower cost at once.

Yellow Tail details are from the authors' own site: it eliminated or reduced
"tannins, complexity, and aging," created "easy drinking, easy to select, and
fun and adventure," targeted American adults who found wine "intimidating and
pretentious," and sold nine times the 25,000 cases expected in its first year.

Be honest about the framework's limits if a student asks. Critics point out the
book's examples were chosen after the fact with no control group, so nobody
knows how many blue ocean attempts failed. It is a lens for finding options. It
proves nothing about whether a given option will work.

For a small business the useful question is the noncustomer one: who needs what
you do and currently buys from nobody like you, and what is putting them off?

## Where they break

The slide Eric asked for: concrete misapplications, from the people who made or
studied each framework.

**Five Forces.** Porter's 2008 article has a "Common Pitfalls" sidebar. His full
list: defining the industry too broadly or too narrowly; making lists instead of
engaging in rigorous analysis; paying equal attention to all of the forces
rather than digging deeply into the most important ones; confusing effect (price
sensitivity) with cause (buyer economics); using static analysis that ignores
industry trends; confusing cyclical or transient changes with true structural
changes; and using the framework to declare an industry attractive or
unattractive rather than to guide strategic choices. The growth trap on the
slide is from the body text: "A common mistake is to assume that fast-growing
industries are always attractive... some fast-growth businesses, such as
personal computers, have been among the least profitable industries in recent
years. A narrow focus on growth is one of the major causes of bad strategy
decisions." The small-business version: picking a business because the category
is booming, when nothing stops fifty others from doing the same.

**SWOT.** Hill and Westbrook, "SWOT Analysis: It's Time for a Product Recall,"
*Long Range Planning*, 1997. They studied 50 UK manufacturers, 20 of which had
done a SWOT, most with paid consultants. Findings, verbatim: "long lists (over
40 factors on average), general (often meaningless) descriptions, a failure to
prioritize and no attempt to verify any points. But the most worrying general
characteristic was that no-one subsequently used the outputs within the later
stages of the strategy process." Items were "often only three- or four-word
phrases" such as "poor quality" and "not innovative enough." "In only three
instances was the SWOT output used" afterwards, and one company could no longer
find its SWOT at all. The customer example is theirs: a food company with one
customer taking more than half its output listed "the value of our contract with
X" as a strength and "over-reliance on company X" as a weakness, and neither the
client nor the consultant asked what followed. Their conclusion: this "does not
constitute analysis at all," because it does "not go beyond description."

**Blue Ocean.** From the authors' own FAQ: "Blue ocean strategy is not about
being first to market. Rather it is about being first to get it right by linking
innovation to value." And from Michael Olenick of the INSEAD Blue Ocean Strategy
Institute: "Segmentation of a niche, focusing on a small group of as-is
customers, is the opposite of blue ocean strategy."

The unifying line on the slide, filling in the boxes and treating that as the
answer, is this deck's synthesis. It is supported by Porter's "making lists
instead of engaging in rigorous analysis" and Hill and Westbrook's "do not go
beyond description."

When a student shows you a SWOT, check three things: is each item backed by
evidence, is each strength measured against a named competitor, and does the
list end in a decision. If the answer to the last one is no, it is a list.

## More than one lens

Eric's point that one framework is rarely enough, earned by the pitfalls slide.

Each blind spot is real and follows from what the lens is for. Five Forces is an
industry tool and says nothing about one company's capabilities. SWOT is a
snapshot, and its items assume the business keeps competing the way it does now.
Blue Ocean looks for noncustomers and says little about defending the customers
you have.

The example in the heads-up is the practical payoff. If SWOT says "our delivery
speed is a strength" and Five Forces says entry is easy and rivals copy anything
within a season, the two lenses have found something neither would alone. It
lines up with Porter's 1996 argument that being better at the same activities is
copyable and does not count as strategy.

The supporting idea in the literature is Mintzberg, Ahlstrand and Lampel's
*Strategy Safari*, which opens with the blind men and the elephant and argues
each school of strategy sees part of the animal. That framing came from reviews
of the book, not its text, so it stays in these notes and off the slide.

Practical guidance for a student: pick one outward lens and one inward lens as a
minimum.

## Analysis to strategy

Rumelt's "kernel." Verbatim: "1. A diagnosis: an explanation of the nature of
the challenge. A good diagnosis simplifies the often overwhelming complexity of
reality by identifying certain aspects of the situation as being the critical
ones. 2. A guiding policy: an overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome
the obstacles identified in the diagnosis. 3. Coherent actions: steps that are
coordinated with one another to support the accomplishment of the guiding
policy."

**This is the hinge of the session.** Everything before it (the lenses) feeds
box one. Everything after it (the walkthrough) is read as boxes one, two and
three. If a student takes one structural idea away, this is it.

His own worked example is Nvidia: diagnosis "we are losing the performance
race", guiding policy of releasing a faster chip three times faster than the
industry norm, coherent actions of three overlapping development teams and
heavy investment in simulation.

The commonest student mistake is doing a framework and believing they are
finished. A completed SWOT is, at best, part of a diagnosis.

## Diagnosis to decisions

**PLACEHOLDER: Nike.** Both cards paraphrase the sample document's "Key Insights
that Shaped Strategic Decisions" (left) and the three named decisions in its
executive summary (right): "Performance-First Innovation Focus," "Balanced
Premium Channel Strategy," and "Prioritized Growth Market Focus."

The teaching move is that **each choice answers a finding.** Rivals taking share
in running leads to performance first. Direct selling hurting retailers leads to
balanced channels. China falling leads to prioritized markets. Trace one of
those lines out loud. That is what "coherent" means.

The second move is the cost. A choice that costs nothing is not a choice. Going
lean in China means accepting less growth there, and the document says so.

Two claims in the sample document are **not** on the slide because they could
not be confirmed: that Nike's global share fell "from 30% to 27%", and that On
and Hoka hold "the #1 spot in running." Circana's 2025 data has Brooks and Hoka
at about 23% each of US adult running, with Nike third. If Eric reads those
lines aloud from the document, this is the caveat.

For reference, the real Nike reset matches the document closely. On his first
earnings call (December 2024) Elliott Hill said "We lost our obsession with
sport," that Nike had "become far too promotional," and that "being premium also
means full price."
