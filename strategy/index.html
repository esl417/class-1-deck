Nike, Inc. · Strategic Blueprint for the Performance-First Reset

The three decisions

Sell through retail partners and direct, where it had been betting on direct alone. Put the innovation money into performance sport. Grow North America hard and run Greater China lean.

Look at that last section. A strategy names what it will stop chasing, and that one line is how you tell it from a to-do list.

Why it feels opaque

A plan is not a strategy.

If you ever wrote a business plan, you probably never opened it again. You were right. Nobody taught you the difference, and most of the advice was written for companies with a planning department.

What a plan usually is
  • A mission statement.
  • A long list of initiatives, as long as the budget allows.
  • A spreadsheet that prices the list.
What a strategy is
  • A few choices about where you'll win.
  • What you will not do, and why.
  • A bet you could turn out to be wrong about.
"The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do."Michael Porter, Harvard Business Review
The value

Why big companies pay for this.

Large companies keep a full-time strategy team, usually about ten people working next to the CEO. This is what they spend their days on.

Where to grow

Finding the next market, product or customer worth going after. 84% of strategy chiefs own this.

Where the money goes

Which bets get funded, which get cut, and what to buy or sell. 82% run planning and deals.

What's coming

Watching competitors and long-term shifts that nobody inside a single department is paid to watch.

💸

They pay because a wrong choice stays expensive for years. In 2020 Nike bet on selling direct and pulled back from retailers. In fiscal 2025 revenue fell 10%, and the new CEO's first job was undoing it.

You make the same kind of choice they do, with far less room to be wrong. Figures: BCG survey of chief strategy officers.

The one-minute test

Spot a bad one in one minute.

Richard Rumelt spent a career reading corporate strategies and found the bad ones fail in the same four ways.

  • Fluff. Big words restating the obvious. One bank's strategy was "customer-centric intermediation." Translation: we're a bank.
  • No problem named. A strategy is a way through a difficulty. If it never says what's hard, it's a wish.
  • Goals dressed up as strategy. "Grow 20% this year" is a destination with no route.
  • A long to-do list. One city's plan had 47 strategies and 178 action items. Number 122 was "create a strategic plan."
⏱️

Run it on your own plan right now. Adjectives where the choices should be, and nothing anyone agreed to stop doing, are the two fastest tells.

The other half of the test

No plan, no measure, no strategy.

Pass those four and you have good choices. A strategy is only as good as its implementation plan, so two more things have to be true.

Someone is doing it

Every choice has an owner, a budget and a date. A choice nobody is funded to carry out is an opinion.

You can see it moving

Every choice has a number you check. One early signal that shows up in weeks, and one result that shows up in quarters.

📉

This is where it breaks, even at big companies. In a survey of 7,600 managers, only 55% of middle managers could name even one of their company's top five priorities. Only 11% said every priority had the people and money it needed.

Sull, Homkes and Sull, Harvard Business Review, 2015.

The frameworks

Three lenses, three questions.

A framework is a question somebody smart already worked out how to ask. There are dozens, and each one gets used for more than one job. These are three you'll meet most often.

Five Forces

"Why is this market hard?"

Looks outward, at the whole industry you're in.

SWOT

"Where do we stand?"

Looks at you and your surroundings, side by side.

Blue Ocean

"Who isn't buying yet?"

Looks for demand that nobody in your industry is serving.

🔎

None of them hands you the answer. Each one makes you look somewhere you weren't looking.

Lens one · Michael Porter

Five Forces: why is this hard?

New entrants
How easily can someone open next door?
Suppliers
Can they raise your costs?
Rivals
How hard do they fight on price?
Buyers
Can they push your price down?
Substitutes
What else does the same job?

There's profit in your industry. Five pressures decide who keeps it. The lens shows you where it leaks out, and to whom.

For a neighborhood coffee shop

Anyone can open a cafe. Customers switch for fifty cents. The substitute is the machine on their own counter. That's three strong forces, and it explains the margins better than "we need more marketing."

Use it to decide whether to enter a market, where to position inside one, and what you need to defend against.

Lens two · Stanford Research Institute, 1960s

SWOT: where do we stand?

Inside the business
Strengths
What you do better than a named rival.
Weaknesses
Where that rival beats you.
Outside it
Opportunities
Changes you could use.
Threats
Changes that could hurt.

It's a fit check. Does what you're good at line up with what's happening around you?

What it's for

A fast, shared read of the situation before a decision. It's the lens most teams reach for first, because everyone can fill it in.

🧾

The original made every manager back each item with evidence. That part got lost, and you'll see what it cost in a minute.

Lens three · W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne

Blue Ocean: who isn't buying?

Eliminate
What does the industry assume that customers don't need?
Reduce
What's overbuilt?
Raise
What should be far better than standard?
Create
What has nobody offered?

The first two lenses help you compete. This one asks whether you could stop competing, by serving people your whole industry ignores.

Yellow Tail wine

It dropped the tannins, the aging and the jargon. It added easy drinking and easy choosing, for people who found wine intimidating. It sold nine times its first-year forecast.

The authors' definition: pursuing differentiation and low cost at the same time. Cutting things is what pays for the new thing. Sales figure from Kim and Mauborgne.

Common misuse

Where people break them.

Five Forces · chasing growth

"It's booming, so it's attractive." A fast-growing market with nothing keeping people out pulls everyone in. Porter's own example: personal computers grew fast and were among the least profitable industries around.

SWOT · the list nobody uses

A study of 20 companies found lists averaging over 40 items, most three or four words long. Only 3 ever used the result again. One listed its biggest customer as a strength, and depending on that customer as a weakness. Nobody connected the two.

Blue Ocean · just a niche

A smaller pond is still the same fight. So is getting there first. The authors mean people who buy from nobody in your industry today.

🧩

It's the same mistake three times: filling in the boxes and treating the filled-in boxes as the answer.

Porter, HBR 2008. Hill and Westbrook, Long Range Planning, 1997. Kim and Mauborgne.

Using them together

One lens isn't enough.

Every lens has a blind spot, and it sits exactly where another one looks.

Five Forces
Sees the whole market, and knows nothing about you.
SWOT
Sees you, as of today, competing the way you already compete.
Blue Ocean
Sees the customers the other two never count.
⚖️

When two lenses disagree, you've found something. A strength on your SWOT that Five Forces says any rival can copy was never a strength.

Guiding policy

The lenses fill in the first box. The strategy is the other two. Rumelt: strategy is "coherent action backed by an argument."

Today's example

Nike. A company you already know, in the middle of a turnaround you can check against the news.

The lens it used

Playing to Win, a fourth framework. Its question is "where will we play, and how will we win?" A different lens doing the same job: feeding the diagnosis.

stratengineai.com

Reading it · boxes one and two

What's hard, then what it chose.

Diagnosis · what it found
  • Younger running brands are taking share.
  • Selling direct damaged the retailers and piled up inventory.
  • Tariffs and constant discounts are eating the margin.
  • Greater China is falling fast, and it's volatile.
Guiding policy · what it chose
  • Performance first. Innovation money goes to sport.
  • Balanced channels. Retail partners and direct together.
  • Prioritized markets. Press in North America, go lean in China.
🎯

Every choice answers a finding, and every choice costs something. Going lean in China means accepting less growth there. That trade is how you know it's a strategy.

Reading it · box three

Every move has a number on it.

Seven initiatives in three priority tiers, and every one is written in the same five parts. This is the second one.

The moveRebuild the retail partnerships.
WhySelling direct alone left gaps in reach and hurt the margin.
HowFormal agreements with Dick's, JD Sports and Nordstrom, with agreed product allocation and shared marketing.
It's working whenWholesale revenue grows 10% a year by the end of fiscal 2027, and retailer sentiment and inventory turns improve before that.
It costs$175M a year, and a dedicated channel team.
What could go wrongThe brand gets diluted. So partners and Nike's own stores carry different product.
🗓️

Then it sequences all seven: the first 3 months, 3 to 9, 9 to 18, and beyond, with named executives owning the results. That's the two extra test questions, answered.

What it's for

What it does on Monday.

A finished strategy earns its keep on an ordinary Monday, when something lands on your desk and you have to choose.

What to say yes to

A retailer wants a bigger partnership. Yes, and that was settled months ago.

What to stop doing

Another site-wide sale to hit the month. No, because selling at full price was one of the choices.

Where the next dollar goes

Two projects want the same budget. The performance product gets it.

📈

And it tells you whether it's working before the revenue shows up. Two years into Nike's real turnaround, sales are flat, but running has grown at double digits for over a year. Without that early number, "not working" and "not yet" look identical.
